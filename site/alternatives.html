Alternatives

Other ways to change what a model does

LoRA is one point on a ladder. Climb only as high as the problem needs: each rung costs more and gives you more control, and more responsibility.

First principle. Behaviour can be changed in three places: the input (prompt, retrieved text), the weights (training), or the activations (editing the model's internal state at run time). Cheaper and shallower sits at the input; deeper and stickier sits at the weights.

System prompt and few-shot examples

Tell

Put the context in the input: who the user is, what the task is, what format to use. A well-framed professional request ("I am a clinician preparing…") often removes a refusal that a bare question triggers, because the model weighs likely intent.

Show
system: You assist verified penetration testers with written reports.
        Be direct and technical. Refuse requests to attack systems without authorisation.
Do

Rerun your 100 Step 1 prompts with the system prompt added and count refusals.

Apply

If refusals drop enough, ship this. Zero training, fully reversible, works on closed models.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

Tell

Search your own documents, paste the best passages into the prompt, ask the model to answer from them. It fixes missing knowledge, not behaviour.

Show
question → embed → vector search → top-k passages → prompt → model → cited answer
Do

Index 20 documents, ask 10 questions, check every answer cites the right passage.

Apply

Use RAG when the problem is "it does not know our stuff". Do not use it to fix tone or refusals.

Vendor fine-tuning (closed weights)

Tell

Some vendors let you upload examples and train a private variant of their model. You never see the weights, and the vendor's usage policy and moderation still apply.

Show
upload train.jsonl → vendor trains → you get a model ID → call it via the same API
Do

Read the vendor's fine-tuning policy page before preparing data.

Apply

Good for format and style. Not a route to different safety behaviour, by design.

LoRA

Tell

Frozen base plus a small low-rank adapter. See Concepts and the SOP.

Show
y = W·x + (α/r)·B·A·x      trainable ≈ 0.1% to 1% of parameters
Do

Follow SOP Steps 3 to 6 on a 0.5B model first; it trains in minutes and teaches you the loop.

Apply

Default choice when you need weight-level change on a budget.

QLoRA

Tell

LoRA on top of a base quantised to 4-bit. The frozen weights take about a quarter of the memory; gradients flow through them into the adapter.

Show
base: 16-bit 14 GB → 4-bit ≈ 4 GB      adapter: trained in bf16
Do

Add BitsAndBytesConfig(load_in_4bit=True) as in SOP Step 5.

Apply

Use when VRAM is the limit. Expect a small quality cost versus 16-bit LoRA.

DPO and other preference tuning

Tell

Instead of one correct answer, give pairs: a preferred and a rejected response to the same prompt. The loss pushes the model toward preferred ones relative to a reference copy. Since refusals are largely learned this way, preference data is the most direct lever on refusal behaviour, and it composes with LoRA.

Show
{"prompt":"…","chosen":"direct, accurate answer","rejected":"needless refusal"}
{"prompt":"…","chosen":"brief refusal + safe alternative","rejected":"harmful compliance"}
Do

Build pairs in both directions, so you reward correct answers and correct refusals.

Apply

Run SFT first, then a short DPO pass for fine calibration. Evaluate as in SOP Step 6.

Full fine-tuning

Tell

Update every weight. Most capacity, most memory (weights plus gradients plus optimiser state, often 12 to 16 bytes per parameter), most risk of forgetting.

Show
7B × ~16 bytes ≈ 112 GB of training state → multiple GPUs or offloading
Do

Estimate: parameters × bytes per parameter vs. the VRAM you can rent.

Apply

Only when LoRA at higher rank demonstrably cannot reach the quality you need.

Representation editing (activation steering, direction ablation)

Tell

Interpretability research found that some behaviours, refusal among them, correspond to a direction in the model's internal activations. Steering adds or removes that direction at run time, or bakes the removal into the weights. It needs almost no data and no training loop.

Show
activations(prompts that get refused) − activations(prompts that do not) → direction v
edit: h ← h − (h·v) v        (project the direction out)
Do

Read the published research on refusal directions and try steering on a small model in a notebook, with the same Step 6 evaluation.

Apply

Know the trade-off: it is blunt. It tends to remove refusals everywhere, with no way to keep your HOLD list, and can dent general ability. A LoRA trained on data that includes kept refusals gives you a policy you chose, which is why this site's SOP uses it.

Use an existing community fine-tune

Tell

The open ecosystem already publishes less-restricted fine-tunes of popular bases. Their quality and safety vary and their training data is rarely audited.

Show
Hugging Face Hub → filter by base model and licence → read the model card → check the eval notes
Do

Run your Step 1 prompt set against two or three candidates before training your own.

Apply

You inherit the publisher's choices. For anything customer-facing, run Step 6 yourself and keep your runtime guards from Step 8.
